System 01 · cortical physiology

REM cortex & EEG

REM sleep often shows low-voltage, mixed-frequency scalp EEG, but dreaming is not exclusive to REM. Modern studies also find dream experience in NREM, with posterior cortical activity linked to whether and what people report dreaming.

What “wake-like” does and does not mean

Human REM sleep often shows low-voltage mixed-frequency scalp EEG compared with slow-wave sleep, but saying that “the cortex is awake” is an oversimplification. Dreaming also occurs during NREM sleep, so sleep stage is physiological context rather than a dream decoder.

Dreaming, memory, and emotion

Sleep contributes to memory and affective regulation, and REM is relevant to several emotional processes. What a particular dream means, however, cannot be read directly from those system-level findings. A single dream report does not identify which memory process or emotional operation was active.

Do not infer pruning or rehearsal from the plot

Synaptic homeostasis, memory reorganization, and emotional processing are active research areas. A bizarre, compressed, threatening, or repetitive dream is not evidence that one particular mechanism was occurring.

Neural correlates of dream experience

High-density EEG research has associated reports of dreaming in both REM and NREM sleep with local changes in posterior cortical activity. These are neural correlates measured around dream reports, not a method for decoding an individual dream scene from its narrative.

Human evidence: Siclari et al., Nature Neuroscience (2017), PMID 28394322.

Record sleep and dream context

Treat dream recall, timing, and waking observations as separate data. A narrative cannot reconstruct the sleeper's EEG or reveal one specific memory or emotional mechanism.
