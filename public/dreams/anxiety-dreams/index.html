Anxiety Dreams and Panic in Sleep

A racing heart, a wave of dread, a public-exposure scenario with no clear cause — anxiety dreams don't always need a monster or a chase to feel intensely, physically real.

SIGNAL

REM's amygdala-driven autonomic arousal (heart rate, breathing, adrenaline signaling) can run at high intensity independent of a coherent narrative — the fear is often the primary event, with the storyline built around it.

What's actually happening

In most threat dreams (chases, snakes, attacks), the narrative and the physiological arousal build together. Pure anxiety dreams sometimes run the arousal first — a genuine autonomic nervous system spike, heart rate and breathing shifting, adrenaline-adjacent signaling active — with a comparatively thin or generic narrative wrapped around it after the fact. This is a reasonable account of why some anxiety dreams feel intensely frightening despite very little actually happening in them: the fear is real and physiologically driven; the plot is almost incidental.

This also explains why anxiety dreams so often carry over into waking physical sensation — a racing heart or shallow breathing that persists for a minute or two after you're fully awake. The autonomic nervous system doesn't distinguish cleanly between 'this happened in a dream' and 'this happened,' and takes a short while to downregulate regardless of which one it was.

Public-exposure and being-afraid-of-someone-familiar variants both draw on the same underlying arousal system, differing mainly in which social fear template gets attached to it — visibility and judgment in the first case, safety and trust in the second.
