REM physiology · evidence and limits

REM sleep mechanics

REM sleep combines activated cortical patterns, marked skeletal-motor inhibition, phasic and autonomic variability, and a changing distribution across the night. Dreaming also occurs in NREM sleep, so a dream report alone cannot identify one REM mechanism.

Measure physiology; keep interpretation separate

Sleep stage, muscle activity, EEG, breathing, and autonomic signals can be measured with appropriate methods. Subjective recall can describe an experience, but it cannot reconstruct an individual EEG, quantify atonia, identify a PGO event, or prove the mechanism behind a dream scene.

Four reviewed layers

REM commonly shows low-voltage mixed-frequency scalp EEG, but dreaming is not exclusive to REM. EEG findings describe brain state and correlates of experience; they do not provide a scene-by-scene dream decoder.

Cortex & EEG evidence →

REM normally includes marked reduction of skeletal-motor output. Normal twitches, sleep paralysis, objectively measured REM sleep without atonia, and REM sleep behavior disorder are related topics but are not interchangeable.

REM atonia evidence →

PGO waves are strongly characterized in animal research and PGO-like phasic potentials have been recorded directly in the human pons. Their precise contribution to human dream imagery remains uncertain. REM autonomic variability is real, but a racing heart or unusual breathing sensation does not identify a PGO event.

PGO & autonomic evidence →

REM tends to occupy more time in later sleep cycles, but cycle duration varies between people and nights. Clock time can provide context; it cannot diagnose an individual's sleep stage.
